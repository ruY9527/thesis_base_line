Abstract. Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) promises material-level percep-
tion beyond RGB vision, yet current models remain tightly coupled to
sensing hardware. Minor changes in spectral discretization or spatial res-
olution can invalidate trained networks, despite all sensors observing the
same material reflectance physics. We introduce OneHSI, a unified hy-
perspectral foundation model that aligns representations across hetero-
geneous sensing configurations through physically motivated inductive
biases. Rather than treating bands and resolutions as arbitrary tensor di-
mensions, OneHSI reparameterizes spectral channels in a shared continu-
ous wavelength space, separates scale-invariant relational attention from
scale-conditioned feature transformation, and balances cross-domain con-
tributions to learn sensor-invariant representations. Cross-domain exper-
iments demonstrate state-of-the-art performance and strong robustness
under sensor and scale shifts, supporting physics-aligned representation
learning as a principled path toward cross-sensor generalization.
Keywords: Hyperspectral imaging · Foundation model · Cross-sensor
generalization · Spectral representation learning

Introduction
Hyperspectral imaging (HSI) promises material-level perception beyond RGB
vision [24,64], supporting applications from satellite crop monitoring [60,62] to
intraoperative tumor assessment [42,58]. Across these settings, sensing configura-
tions differ dramatically, spanning hundreds of meters to micrometers in spatial
scale [93,106], with varying spectral bands [25,71], wavelength coverage [27,94],
and noise characteristics [67, 83]. Consequently, models trained on one sensor
often generalize poorly on another [37, 86, 96], trapping hyperspectral learning
in a device-dependent paradigm where minor hardware changes can invalidate
trained models and compromise reproducibility [69,92].

Recent efforts attempt to extend Vision Foundation Models (VFMs) [61,101,
113] to hyperspectral imagery, including SpectralGPT [38], HyperSIGMA [99],
SpectralEarth [12], and HyperFree [46]. However, these approaches are predomi-
nantly trained on relatively homogeneous satellite imagery and implicitly assume
stable spectral configurations and spatial scales [96,119]. As a result, when de-
ployed in ground-level scenarios such as medical imaging [42,58] or autonomous
driving [29,105], where spectral bands, spatial resolutions, and acquisition condi-
tions differ substantially, their performance often degrades significantly [10,88].
Although remote and ground-level hyperspectral systems differ in observation
geometry and spatial scale (as illustrated in Fig. 1), their measurements share
a common physical basis: intrinsic spectral signatures are largely gov-
erned by material composition rather than the sensing device [15,24,64].
Acquisition-dependent factors such as spectral mixing, atmospheric and medium
effects, and sensor noise do vary with scale and platform; nonetheless, the relative
spectral shape (band ratios, peak structure) remains broadly informative across
sensors, which can be viewed as sampling related spectra with different wave-
length responses and physical resolutions [25,89]. Yet current learning pipelines
consistently fail to exploit this physical commonality. Three primary factors
fundamentally break this cross-sensor consistency: (i) spectral misalignment
across sensor-specific wavelength coordinates (C1); (ii) spatial-scale incon-
sistency caused by heterogeneous resolutions (C2); and (iii) data imbalance
that biases representation learning toward dominant domains (C3).
To improve physical consistency, we design OneHSI, a unified hyperspec-
tral foundation model that promotes invariance to sensing configurations by
aligning representations with the underlying material physics. Specifically, at
spectral level, Spectral Coordinate Reparameterization (SCORE) repa-
rameterizes band heterogeneity in a shared continuous wavelength space, learn-

– We build on the premise that hyperspectral observations across satellites and
ground-level sensors share a common reflectance structure largely governed
by material composition, despite large variations in spectral configurations

representation learning.
– We design OneHSI with three modules: (i) Spectral Coordinate Reparameteri-

conditions feature transformation on physical scale, and (iii) Cross-Domain

domain sampling and adversarial alignment.
– Comprehensive experiments across heterogeneous hyperspectral domains show
that OneHSI achieves state-of-the-art performance while remaining robust
to sensor shifts, scale variations, and domain imbalance, demonstrating the
benefit of physics-aligned representation learning.

Related Work
Deep Learning for Hyperspectral Imaging. Deep learning has significantly
advanced HSI analysis [48,74,92], but progress remains highly domain-specific.
Most methodological development has focused on remote sensing [43], supported
by large-scale benchmarks and practical demand. This has led to increasingly so-
phisticated spectral–spatial CNNs [112] and Transformer-based architectures [16,
32,33,57,111], and more recently, Mamba-style state space models [50,100,104].
In contrast, ground-level applications, including proximal sensing [13,82], med-
ical imaging [80, 91], and autonomous driving [6, 29], remain data-limited and
fragmented. Models developed for satellite imagery often fail to transfer to fine-
grained ground-level scenes, exposing a persistent domain gap [18,53,108].

intrinsic heterogeneity in sensing configurations [79]. At the sensor level, dif-

Hyperspectral Foundation Models. Inspired by VFMs (e.g., ViT [17], MAE [34],
and CLIP [81]), recent works extend large-scale pre-training to hyperspectral
imagery [61, 101, 113]. Models such as SpectralGPT [38], HyperSIGMA [99],
and SpectralEarth [12] adopt the Masked Autoencoder (MAE) paradigm [34] to
learn spectral–spatial representations from remote sensing datasets, while Hyper-
Free [46] explores prompt-based segmentation pre-training. Despite their strong
performance, these models are predominantly trained on satellite imagery and
implicitly assume fixed spectral bands and spatial scales, limiting their ability to
generalize across heterogeneous sensors. While concurrent works pursue broader
generalization across spectral channels [44] or cameras [7], OneHSI instead ad-
dresses spectral, spatial-scale, and domain heterogeneity jointly.

3.2
Overview of OneHSI
As illustrated in Fig. 2, we introduce OneHSI, a unified hyperspectral founda-
tion model with physically motivated inductive biases that addresses coordi-
nate mismatch at three structural levels: spectral, spatial, and domain. At the
spectral level, Spectral Coordinate Reparameterization (SCORE) lifts discrete
channels into a shared continuous wavelength space via wavelength-conditioned
projections (Sec. 3.3). At the spatial level, Scale-Equivariant Adaptive Trans-
formation (SEAT) decouples relational modeling from scale-specific adaptation:
shared self-attention preserves long-range spectral–spatial dependencies, while
resolution-aware experts modulate feed-forward transformations (Sec. 3.4). At
the domain level, Cross-Domain Representation Equalization (CORE) balances
per-domain contributions and, together with a domain-adversarial objective,
learns sensor-invariant representations (Sec. 3.5).
3.3
Spectral COordinate REparameterization (SCORE)

nates, so channel indices lack a consistent physical meaning across domains [36,
96]. Rather than aligning discrete indices or inverting the imaging process,
SCORE reparameterizes each spectral channel as a sample of a shared con-
tinuous function over wavelength.

3.4
Scale-Equivariant Adaptive Transformation (SEAT)
Physical scale alters local spatial frequency characteristics but preserves long-
range relational structure [22,52]. This suggests that scale invariance primarily
concerns relational dependencies rather than local feature statistics. In trans-
formers, such relational dependencies are naturally modeled by self-attention,
whereas feed-forward layers refine localized transformations [76,95]. We therefore
introduce the SEAT, which shares self-attention layers across domains and con-
ditions only the feed-forward components on physical scale [23, 77]. Intuitively,
two views of the same material at different resolutions share the same relations
among regions but may need different local filters; SEAT therefore keeps atten-
tion shared while only scale-conditioned experts adapt the feed-forward path. We
do not assume semantic equivalence across scales: the experts absorb the (pos-
sibly non-smooth) scale-dependent variation through smooth Gaussian gating
over log10 s.

3.5
Cross-Domain Representation Equalization (CORE)
As shown in Table 1, the hyperspectral data distribution is highly skewed across
domains, where remote sensing datasets significantly outnumber those from
ground-level domains. Such imbalance biases gradient updates toward dominant
domains, potentially skewing the shared representation and degrading cross-
domain invariance [4, 9]. To mitigate domain-induced representation collapse,
CORE enforces balanced domain contribution at the mini-batch level. Specif-
ically, given M coarse domain pools (remote/proximal/medical/driving), each
mini-batch B is constructed by uniformly sampling Kdom instances per domain:
  |\ m athca
l { B}_ m | = K_{dom},\quad \forall m\in \{1,\dots ,M\}. 
(12)
This yields |B| = MKdom with uniform coarse-domain proportions and enforces
a uniform per-domain proportion of 1/M, which helps stabilize cross-domain
optimization [21,28]. In short, CORE operates at the data level through balanced
per-domain sampling, and is complemented by the domain-adversarial objective
in our training loss, which aligns features across domains; together they prevent
dominant domains from dictating the shared representation.

Pre-training Datasets. To learn a universal hyperspectral representation, we
curate a large-scale, multi-source dataset along three fundamental axes (Table 1).
– Spectral Heterogeneity. The dataset includes measurements from airborne
AVIRIS [25], spaceborne EnMAP [27], and ground-level push-broom sen-
sors [87,109], covering different wavelength configurations and band numbers.
– Scale Diversity. Spatial resolutions span from meter-scale remote sensing [27]
to micrometer-scale microscopy [116], covering multiple orders of magnitude.
– Semantic Diversity. The corpus covers natural landscapes [2,82] urban and
driving environments [29, 107], and biomedical samples [71, 87], preventing
over-specialization to any single domain.
Downstream Datasets. To prevent data contamination, we ensure that the
downstream datasets (Table 1) are strictly disjoint from the pretraining set
(full dataset descriptions, splits, and statistics in Supp. Sec. B). We evalu-
ate OneHSI across four heterogeneous sensing domains—remote sensing, prox-
imal imaging, medical imaging, and driving scenes—covering six representative
hyperspectral tasks: classification (HC), segmentation (HS), object detection
(HOD), change detection (HCD), anomaly detection (HAD), and saliency de-
tection (HSD) (Fig. 3). This evaluation protocol systematically tests cross-sensor
generalization, cross-scale robustness, and cross-task transferability.
Baselines. To comprehensively evaluate OneHSI, we compare against two dis-
tinct categories of SOTA baselines: task-specific models tailored to individual
benchmarks, and general-purpose hyperspectral foundation models.

Task-Specific Models. These include Transformer-based architectures such as
SpectralFormer [37], SpecTr [114], State Space Models (SSMs) tailored for long
spectral sequences (e.g., MambaHSI [50], S2Mamba [100], SS-Mamba [49]), as
well as established CNN-based frameworks (e.g., Unet [85], S2ADet [35]). These
methods are competitive task-specific baselines implemented under their official
protocols and tuned for each task.
Hyperspectral Foundation Models. We compare with recent hyperspectral
foundation models, including HyperSIGMA [99] and HyperFree [46]. For a con-
sistent comparison, all foundation baselines are initialized using their official
pretrained checkpoints and fine-tuned under the same downstream training pro-
tocol. This setup allows us to evaluate cross-domain generalization and represen-
tation efficiency relative to existing unified hyperspectral pretraining approaches.
Metrics. We adopt standard evaluation protocols tailored to each downstream
task category (full per-task metric definitions in Supp. Sec. C). For pixel-level
classification and semantic segmentation tasks, we report Overall Accuracy (OA),
Average Accuracy (AA), and the Kappa coefficient (KA), with mean Intersec-
tion over Union (mIoU) additionally used for segmentation. For object detection
tasks, we report Precision, and mean Average Precision (mAP), where AP@0.5
is computed at an IoU threshold of 0.5 unless otherwise specified. For change
detection and anomaly detection, which typically involve binary or highly im-
balanced settings, we additionally report the F1-score to balance precision and
recall. For salient object detection, we adopt three commonly used structural
evaluation metrics, including the Structure-measure (Sα), Weighted F-measure
(Fβ), and Enhanced-alignment measure (Eξ).
Implementation Details. All experiments are implemented in PyTorch 2.5.0
and trained on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs for approximately 30 hours. We adopt
a ViT-Huge encoder [17] with scale-conditioned MoE (top-Kexp = 2). Training
runs for 100 epochs (1000 iterations each) using AdamW with an initial learning
rate of 2 × 10−4, 5-epoch warmup, and cosine decay. The batch size is B = 8
with balanced cross-domain sampling (M = 4, Kdom = 2), and MAE masking
ratio is 0.75. Loss weights are α = 0.10 and β = 0.05 (complete architecture and
pretraining hyperparameters in Supp. Sec. A).
Downstream adaptation. We adapt the shared pretrained encoder with lightweight
task-specific heads, fine-tuning the encoder for all tasks except anomaly detec-
tion, which uses it frozen; all compared foundation models follow the identical
pipeline, with the full per-task protocol in Supp. Sec. A.10.

Robustness to Metadata and Sensor Noise. We assess robustness to im-
perfect wavelength metadata and sensor noise. Under wavelength jitter OneHSI
stays stable, losing only 0.36 OA at a 5 nm perturbation, and it degrades only
gradually as the metadata assumptions become coarser. Under Gaussian, Poisson–

under realistic acquisition perturbations.

Ablation Studies
Overview. We conduct a series of ablation studies to analyze the key designs
of OneHSI. First, we examine domain composition and training strategies to
highlight the importance of joint multi-domain optimization (Fig. 5). Second,
we evaluate the proposed architectural modules by comparing SCORE with
alternative spectral mappings (Table 5) and SEAT with standard MoE baselines
(Table 7). Third, we study the training objectives, analyzing the effects of the
auxiliary losses (Table 6).
Impact of Domain Composition and Training Strategy. We ablate the
joint multi-domain pre-training to assess the role of domain diversity (Fig. 5).
Removing any single domain consistently degrades performance, with the largest
drops observed on tasks from the corresponding domain; notably, excluding re-
mote sensing data causes the most severe degradation. We further compare joint
training with a sequential training baseline that optimizes on remote, proximal,
medical, and driving data in order. Sequential training underperforms joint train-
ing on most datasets, highlighting the importance of simultaneous multi-domain
exposure for learning domain-balanced spectral representations.

Conclusions
In this work, we tackle the long-standing device dependency in hyperspectral
learning by introducing OneHSI, a physically consistent hyperspectral foun-
dation model. By reparameterizing spectral channels in continuous wavelength
space, separating scale-invariant relational modeling from scale-conditioned adap-
tation, and balancing cross-domain contributions, OneHSI improves consistency
across heterogeneous sensing configurations. Extensive experiments spanning
satellite, proximal, medical, and driving datasets demonstrate that OneHSI achieves
state-of-the-art performance while remaining robust under spectral discretiza-
tion shifts, resolution changes, and domain imbalance. While our current frame-
work assumes reliable wavelength metadata and does not explicitly model atmo-
spheric effects, integrating physics-informed degradation modeling and scaling
to broader sensing regimes remain promising directions. More broadly, this work
suggests that hyperspectral foundation modeling should be built upon physi-
cal invariances rather than sensor-specific statistics, offering a principled path
toward truly cross-sensor material perception.
